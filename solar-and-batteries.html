Solar and batteries: do you need one?

A battery stores the solar power you do not use during the day so you can use it in the evening. Whether that is worth paying for depends on how you use power. Here is how to decide.

Updated 20 October 20263 min readBy Solward

Key takeaways

  • A battery is not essential. EECA notes that solar without a battery will generally give the best returns.
  • EECA’s typical battery cost is $5,000 to $15,000 on top of the solar system, which usually lengthens payback.
  • Batteries make most sense if you use most of your power at night and cannot shift it, or if you want power during an outage.
  • You can design a system to be battery-ready and add storage later.

What a battery actually does

Solar panels make most of their power in the middle of the day, when many households are out. A battery captures the surplus that would otherwise be exported and releases it in the evening, when you are home and power costs most to buy. It does not make more electricity. It moves it to when you need it.

$5k–$15ktypical added cost of a home battery (EECA)
5 · 10 · 20 kWhbattery sizes EECA pairs with 3, 5 and 10 kW systems

When a battery earns its place

  • Most of your power use is in the evening and overnight, and you cannot shift it.
  • You want some of your home to keep running during a power cut.
  • You are on, or moving to, a time-of-use plan or an export plan that pays more at peak times.
  • You are adding an EV or heat pump and expect evening load to grow.

When solar alone is the better spend

If someone is usually home in the day, or you can run the dishwasher, washing and hot water in daylight, your panels already supply power when you need it. Spending the same money on a slightly larger solar system can pay back faster. EECA’s guidance is the same: a battery lengthens payback, and is best considered when night-time use is high or backup matters.

Sizing, in plain terms

Solar systems are measured in kilowatts (kW), which is how fast they can generate. Batteries are measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), which is how much they can store. EECA’s typical examples pair a 5 kWh battery with a 3 kW system, 10 kWh with 5 kW, and 20 kWh with 10 kW. Your evening use, not the size of your roof, should set the battery size.

Backup power is a separate question

Many people assume a battery keeps the lights on in a blackout. It only does so if the system is designed and wired for it, usually with a dedicated backup circuit covering the loads you choose. If that matters to you, say so at the start. We set out what would and would not run, in writing, before anything is ordered.

Start battery-ready

Plenty of households install panels first and add storage once they understand their usage and buy-back rates. If that suits you, choose a system designed for it: an inverter that can work with a battery, a switchboard with room, and a roof layout that will not need to change later. Peak-time export rewards, introduced in 2026, are one more reason to keep the option open. See Where solar is going.

The honest answer

For many homes, solar alone is the better first step. We will show you both options in your quote so you can compare the numbers side by side.
