Why go solar? An honest look at the case for it

Solar is a long-term decision, so the reasons should hold up to scrutiny. Here is the case for it, the numbers behind it, and the situations where it is not the right call.

Updated 20 October 20263 min readBy Solward

  2. Payback is a range, not a promise
  3. What happens to the power you do not use

  5. Resilience and control

Key takeaways

  • Solar saves money by cutting what you buy from the grid. The more of your own power you use, the more it saves.
  • EECA estimates an Auckland home with a 5 kW system could save about $1,246 a year, with a typical payback of 7 to 10 years when most of the power is used at home.
  • Payback depends on how and when you use power. A home that is empty all day will see a longer payback than one that is busy in daylight hours.
  • It is a poor fit if your roof needs work soon, or if you cannot comfortably wait years for the upfront cost to come back.

It cuts what you buy from the grid

A solar system makes electricity in daylight. Whatever your home uses at that moment is power you do not buy from your retailer. That is where most of the value comes from, which is why the shape of your day matters as much as the size of the system.

EECA, the government’s energy efficiency agency, estimated in December 2025 that a 5 kW system on an Auckland home with electric heating and hot water, and average power use, could save about $1,246 a year. The same exercise for other regions gave figures between roughly $1,050 and $1,470.

Payback is a range, not a promise

EECA puts the typical payback at 7 to 10 years for a household that can use most of what its panels produce. Shorter paybacks are possible in sunnier regions with higher power prices. Longer ones, 10 to 12 years, are common when less of the power is used at home. Those figures also leave out any interest if you borrow to pay for the system.

We would rather show you a range with the assumptions written down than quote a single figure. The system shaper on our Residential page does exactly that.

What happens to the power you do not use

Surplus power flows to the grid and your retailer credits you for it, typically at a lower rate than you pay to buy electricity. That is why designing a system around your own daytime use, rather than around export, tends to pay better. The rules for exports are changing, and we cover them in Where solar is going.

It can add value to your home

A recent New Zealand study of home sales found that homes with solar sold for an average premium of 1.34 per cent, a figure EECA cites. It is an average, not a promise, but it means the money is not entirely sunk if you move.

Resilience and control

A solar system on its own does not keep the lights on in a power cut. Grid-connected systems shut down for safety. Add a battery configured with backup and you can keep chosen circuits running. If resilience matters to you, tell us early so we can design for it. Our guide to solar and batteries explains the trade-offs.

When solar is not the right call

  • Your roof needs replacing or major work in the next ten years. Do the roof first, to avoid paying to remove and refit panels.
  • You use very little power in the day and cannot shift it. Savings will be smaller, and a battery changes the cost equation.
  • You are planning renovations that change the roof or wiring. Do that work first, with solar in mind.
  • You still rely heavily on gas. Electrifying hot water and heating first usually makes solar worth more.
  • You are not comfortable with an upfront cost and a wait of several years before it comes back.
A note on numbers

Figures on this page come from published sources and are examples, not guarantees. Your own savings depend on your tariff, usage, roof and system. A free quote shows the assumptions behind every estimate.
