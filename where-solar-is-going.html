Where solar is going in New Zealand

Solar is still a small share of New Zealand’s electricity, but it is growing quickly and the rules around it are being rewritten. Here is what is happening and what it could mean for your system.

Updated 20 October 20264 min readBy Solward

Key takeaways

  • New Zealand’s solar capacity grew by about half in 2025 and is thought to be above 1 GW now.
  • Large solar farms are being built alongside rooftops, with hundreds of megawatts more due over the next few years.
  • 2026 brought rule changes for rooftop systems: a building consent exemption, a default 10 kW export limit, and rewards for exporting at peak times.
  • Policy can change. Designing a system around your own use is the most resilient choice.

A fast-growing, still small, part of the grid

MBIE’s Energy in New Zealand 2026 report puts solar capacity at 830 MW at the end of 2025, up 52 per cent from 545 MW a year earlier. It is likely above 1 GW now. Even so, solar supplies only a small share of the country’s electricity, in the region of two per cent on recent estimates, while hydro, geothermal and wind carry most of the load. In 2025, 88.5 per cent of New Zealand’s electricity came from renewable sources.

Solar farms and rooftops are growing together

Seven utility-scale solar plants of 9 MW or more were switched on in 2025, including the first connected directly to the national transmission grid. The Electricity Authority reports 783 MW of new grid-connected solar due in 2026 and 2027, which would take grid-connected solar to roughly 1,031 MW. On 30 January 2026, solar output reached a half-hourly record of 210 MW.

Rooftops are part of the same story. The Electricity Authority has counted around 75,000 households with solar and roughly 14,700 with batteries.

The rules for rooftop solar are changing

There are no Australian-style subsidies, but 2025 and 2026 brought a run of practical changes:

  • A building consent exemption. In October 2025 the government announced that rooftop solar panels would be exempt from the requirement for a building consent. We confirm what applies to your roof at the site visit.
  • A 10 kW default export limit. The Electricity Authority has told lines companies to offer a default export limit of 10 kW for straightforward household solar and battery systems, with changes staged from late April 2026 through to mid-October.
  • Rewards for exporting at peak times. From 1 April 2026, lines companies must pay rebates for power exported at peak demand. Retailers are expected to pass these on, as a buy-back rate or bill credit, from 1 July 2026. The rebate applies up to an export limit of 45 kW.
  • More time-of-use plans. The Authority expected time-of-use pricing to be available to most New Zealanders by 1 July 2026, which suits households that can shift power use or store it.
  • A review of small-scale solar. In May 2026 the government launched a review aimed at making residential and small-scale solar installations faster and easier.

Batteries are moving from niche to mainstream

The number of households combining solar with a battery has climbed sharply, up 72 per cent between July 2024 and early 2026 according to Electricity Authority data reported in March 2026. Peak-export rewards make storage more attractive, though EECA still notes that solar without a battery generally gives the best returns for most homes. Our guide to solar and batteries goes through when storage makes sense.

What it means for a system you install today

  • Size for your own use first. Savings come mostly from power you use yourself, and that holds whatever happens to export rates.
  • Choose equipment that can grow. A battery-ready inverter and a switchboard with room let you add storage, an EV charger or a heat pump later.
  • Expect the rules to keep moving. Export limits, rebates and plans have all changed within a year. We build the current rules into every quote and flag where they could shift.

What nobody knows yet

Policy and prices can change, and forecasts are only forecasts. EECA’s longer-term modelling points to a further rise in solar generation from the early 2030s under both of its scenarios, but nobody can say precisely how fast. A good system should make sense on today’s numbers, with anything extra as a bonus.
